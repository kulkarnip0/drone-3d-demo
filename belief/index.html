Distributed UAV Intelligence:
Architecture, Differentiation and Validation Roadmap

Technical clarification on our UAS-function software platform and the path toward decentralized multi-UAV operation

01 Architecture02 Technology & IP03 Demonstration & edge
Overview. We are building a platform-independent software layer for UAS functions such as search, surveillance, tracking, mapping and inspection. Our development path begins with a coordinated multi-UAV prototype and progresses toward agents that can share small, meaningful observations and make selected decisions locally. We use open frameworks for flight control and connectivity; our intended differentiation is the mission-intelligence layer above them.

1. From centralized to decentralized coordination

How does Phase 1, where the ground station combines local beliefs into a global belief, evolve into decentralized autonomy? How is this different from leader–follower coordination?

We start from two bottlenecks: delayed decisions and unreliable communication.

In a mobile UAV network, communication is not constant. Bandwidth changes, links drop, and information becomes old while it is being sent or processed. A simple leader–follower design reduces coordination complexity, but makes the followers dependent on the leader and its communications. Our approach is to reduce the information that must be exchanged and, gradually, reduce the number of decisions that must wait for a central point.

Local belief: a description rather than an image

Consider three UAVs inspecting a road. UAV 1 detects a vehicle. UAV 2 does not need to receive its camera frame to decide where to look next. It needs a short description: the vehicle class, position, movement, observation time and confidence. We call this a local belief.

What that means for communication

Suppose 10 UAVs each broadcast one belief update twice per second. The payload and MAVLink framing alone would require approximately 9.12 kbps total, assuming single-hop broadcast and no retransmission.

These are illustrative packet rates—not measured FANET throughput. Real utilization is higher once routing, radio framing, retransmissions, other telemetry and multi-hop forwarding are included. More tracked objects also increase the load.

A smaller message is an important starting point, but it does not by itself solve distributed coordination. We also need to handle stale observations, overlapping detections, incompatible local views and network partitions. Our intended next step is to share only important changes and to let an agent take a safe, pre-authorized action without waiting for every other UAV.

Belief sharing must account for the fact that different UAVs will never have perfectly synchronized views. Each observation carries its timestamp, confidence and position reference. A receiving UAV can discount stale observations, associate reports that refer to the same object, and retain uncertainty when observations conflict. Under link loss, it should execute only tasks already authorized and safe for its current local state; when the link recovers, it can reconcile newer observations with its local history. These are design requirements to be validated, not capabilities claimed as complete.

A three-stage development path

Figure 3 — Progressive removal of central dependencies
Phase 1 — Centralized

UAV observations form local beliefs. The GCS combines them and coordinates tasks. Early inference may run in the cloud.

Phase 2 — Hybrid

UAVs exchange compact beliefs directly; the GCS remains mission supervisor.

Phase 3 — Distributed

Each UAV maintains its own mission view and performs selected tasks locally; no permanent leader is required.

Logical progression, not a claim that all stages are implemented today. Distribution of belief sharing and distribution of decision authority are separate milestones.

Leader–follower makes one UAV the main coordinator. Our proposed distributed system instead uses each UAV's own observations and the beliefs received from peers. It aims to tolerate the loss of one agent, though useful coordination still depends on available connectivity and how safely conflicting decisions are handled.

2. Open-source foundations and our own contributions

Which components are Kshatriya's own, rather than integrations of open-source software? Where could the first patentable contribution arise?

We use open infrastructure where it already solves a standard engineering problem; our intended contribution is the intelligence layer that converts sensor observations into coordinated mission decisions.

PX4/ArduPilot provides flight control; ROS2/DDS provides software messaging; MAVLink provides flight-system interfaces. We do not consider the mere integration of these components proprietary innovation.

Established building blocksProposed Kshatriya development
PX4 / ArduPilot, flight interfacesMission behavior and coordinated task decisions
ROS2 / DDS, MAVLink, networkingCompact local-belief representation and peer updates
Public detectors, trackers and simulatorsCross-UAV belief merging, uncertainty handling and tracking handover
Standard planners and coverage algorithmsPlanning based on where the shared mission picture is incomplete

The proposed technical mechanism has three parts: (i) maintain an uncertainty-aware map of what is known and unknown; (ii) estimate which available UAV and viewpoint can most improve that map; and (iii) allocate a sensing task while considering observation age, geometry, battery, communication cost and safety. We intend to test this against geometric coverage, centralized assignment and leader–follower baselines, measuring useful information gained, time to confirm an observation, link traffic and mission completion.

Our first candidate for an invention disclosure is belief-completion-aware task allocation: choosing which UAV should observe which region based on the expected reduction of missing or uncertain information, while respecting sensor geometry, energy, connectivity and safety constraints.

This is a research direction, not a claim of granted or established novelty. Its patentability depends on prior-art analysis, a sufficiently specific method, and measured improvement over relevant baselines.

3. Demonstration, communication loss and edge deployment

If the 9–12-month demonstration uses cloud inference, what can it show under communication-degraded or GPS-denied conditions, and when does inference move onboard?

The first demonstration validates the decision pipeline—not full operational independence from the cloud.

Our 9–12-month goal is to show sensing, object-level beliefs, a merged mission view and coordinated tasking across multiple simulated or controlled UAV agents. Some AI inference may still depend on cloud connectivity. A cloud-dependent UAV cannot produce new AI detections during an outage unless an onboard fallback model is available; safe flight-controller behavior or completion of a previously authorized bounded task should not be confused with continued autonomous ISR. Communication loss and GNSS loss also require separate tests and mitigations.

In Phase 1, each local belief represents one UAV’s observations, even if inference initially runs in the cloud. Onboard belief generation follows when task-specific models move to edge compute.

Demonstration and expected evidence

We will make the first demonstration measurable by reporting four scenarios separately. The purpose is to show both what the prototype can do and where cloud dependence remains.

DemonstrationExpected evidence
Normal operationMultiple agents produce observations, update beliefs and receive coordinated tasks. Record task execution and end-to-end decision time.
Communication degradationInject packet loss, delay and temporary disconnection. Measure observation age, missed updates, decision delays and recovery after reconnection.
Cloud disconnectionShow which AI functions stop, which onboard flight-control and safety functions continue, and how the system reconnects. Do not claim independent onboard AI unless deployed.
GPS denialRun GNSS-degraded simulation; evaluate position uncertainty and safe responses. This is not evidence of field-proven GPS-denied navigation.

Evolution of edge intelligence

Our goal is not merely to move inference from the cloud to a UAV. It is to progressively increase the intelligence that can operate onboard. Small task-specific models—for object detection, segmentation and tracking—are the first candidates for edge execution. The GCS initially provides the more general intelligence: understanding mission objectives, combining beliefs across UAVs, prioritizing tasks and proposing coordinated actions.

We plan to use the richer ground-side reasoning as a source of demonstrations and training signals. Selected behaviors can then be distilled into smaller onboard models or policies, tested in simulation and deployed incrementally. Each UAV can gradually progress from detecting an object, to maintaining a local belief, to choosing a bounded mission action without continuously asking the GCS.

Figure 4 — Moving selected mission intelligence toward the edge

Target architecture, not a completed deployment. Distillation may involve supervised learning, policy learning and compact model design; successful transfer must be verified on the target edge hardware.

We distinguish three milestones: distributed information (agents exchange compact beliefs), distributed decisions (agents make bounded choices without waiting for the GCS), and distributed intelligence (increasingly sophisticated onboard models and policies). These milestones can progress separately. We do not intend to move every GCS function onboard: broader fleet reasoning and human supervision may remain at the ground station.

Planned sequence

PeriodTarget
0–6 monthsSimulation, sensor interfaces, local-belief schema and initial cloud models
6–12 monthsEnd-to-end demonstration, fault injection and coordination baseline
12–18 monthsMove selected perception and belief updates onto edge hardware; benchmark compute, power and latency
18–24 monthsExpand onboard coordination and direct UAV-to-UAV exchange; undertake controlled field evaluation

These are proposed engineering targets, dependent on funding, hardware access, integration and validation progress. The move to edge compute is a measured transition, not an assumption that models trained in the cloud automatically run within UAV power and thermal budgets.

For the first demonstration we will report four sets of results separately: normal end-to-end function; sensitivity to controlled latency and packet loss (observation age, missed updates and recovery time); behavior after cloud disconnection (functions that stop, safe fallback and reconnection); and simulated GNSS degradation (localization assumptions, estimated uncertainty and safe response). Test thresholds will be defined against the selected UAV platform, compute hardware and communication link rather than asserted in advance.

How we will evaluate progress

We will compare centralized, leader–follower and distributed strategies under the same simulated scenarios. Key measures include task completion, time to useful decision, communication traffic, observation age, tracking continuity, onboard compute cost and safe recovery after link or agent failure. Initial comparisons will establish a baseline; improvements must be demonstrated, not presumed.

In summary: the first milestone demonstrates that the software intelligence pipeline works; later milestones establish how much of that intelligence can operate onboard and remain useful when communications degrade. Our goal is to move from sharing data to sharing meaning, and from centralized coordination to carefully bounded local decisions.
